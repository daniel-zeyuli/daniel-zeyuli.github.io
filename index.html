How I think

I think I notice problems more now

Origin Story
As featured in
Lakefield College School
AI in Action series · 2026

Both of my projects started the same way: I noticed friction that everyone around me had quietly accepted as just the way things are.

The laundry app came from a familiar boarding school situation. Students in residence had no way to know whether a machine was free, who was using it, or when a load would finish. Clothes would appear outside machines mid-cycle with no explanation. Nobody was being malicious — there was just no system. "I noticed there was an existing problem in our residence. The laundry was always busy, and you didn't know whose clothes were in the machine. I felt like there was something that needed to be fixed."

I built a reservation app. Students could book time and see machine status in real time. The friction disappeared. And something shifted in how I saw problems. "During the process, I learned a lot, and I helped people. It felt like an all-win situation."

"After I finished that project, I thought — this is so useful. I created something I never thought possible."

The Chrome extension came from a teacher. Ms. Nichols, our Math and CS department head, was frustrated: every time she copied equations from ChatGPT or Claude into Google Docs, the formatting broke. She was paying for a tool to handle this. I looked at what she was paying for and thought it seemed like something I could build.

The first version took about ten hours — not just asking AI to write code, but understanding why formulas were breaking in the first place. "Every language model has a different way of outputting math equations. I had to identify the format, test what worked, and then keep improving it." The extension is now on version 4, supporting equations, chemical formulas, images, charts, and full conversation exports across ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. 77 installs. 41 weekly active users.

Both projects taught me the same thing: "Before, I might think there isn't a solution, so I'll just go to someone else. Now I think there must be a solution, and I can find it." The problems worth solving aren't the ones people complain about. They're the ones people have stopped complaining about — because they've forgotten a better version is possible.
